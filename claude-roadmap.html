Turn saved AI tutorials into something you use

Pick one small project, set a clear outcome and learn as you build. This is a practical roadmap for getting started with Claude, from beginner to pro, in ten steps.

You don't need to do the steps in a rush or all at once. Start with one project and work through the list as you need it.

The rules of the roadmap

One project at a time. A clear outcome before you start. A basic version first. A human check wherever it matters.

Step 1 of 10

Start with Claude

Bring Claude real tasks from your day, not practice exercises. You learn faster when the result matters to you.

How to do it

  1. Pick one real task from this week: a reply you need to write, a plan, a summary or a decision.
  2. Explain the goal: what you want the result to do.
  3. Share the context: who it's for, what you have already tried, anything that affects the answer.
  4. Check what it gives you. Read it critically. Ask what it is unsure about, and fix what is wrong.
  5. Repeat with a different task each day for a week.

Try this:

Prompt
I need to [task]. The goal is [what the result should achieve]. Context: [who it's for and what matters]. What I've already tried: [details]. Give me a first draft, then tell me what you assumed and what you'd want to check.
You're done when

You have used Claude on at least five real tasks and you know how to check its answers.

Watch out

Don't paste confidential client details, passwords or payment information into a chat.

Step 2 of 10

Get better at prompting

The quality of what comes back depends on what you put in. A few habits make a big difference.

How to do it

  1. Include examples. Paste one or two samples of what good looks like.
  2. Add constraints. Length, tone, audience, what to avoid.
  3. Say the format you want: a table, three bullet points, an email, a checklist.
  4. Refine. Read the result, say what is off, and ask again. Treat the first answer as a draft.
  5. Save the prompts that work, so you can reuse them.

Try this:

Prompt template
Task: [what you want]
Audience: [who it's for]
Tone: [for example warm, direct, no hype]
Format: [for example an email under 120 words]
Example of what I like: [paste a sample]
Avoid: [words or styles you don't want]

For tasks that matter, also try the deeper prompt, which makes Claude think about what an expert would do before it rewrites your request.

You're done when

Your prompts regularly give you something you can use after one or two rounds of refining.

Step 3 of 10

Create a Project

Add your documents, tone of voice and instructions in one place, so Claude already knows your business every time you open it.

How to do it

  1. Create a new Project in Claude and name it after your business.
  2. Add the documents Claude should know: your offers, your ideal client, past work, FAQs and samples of your writing.
  3. Write short instructions: who you are, how you like things done and what Claude should never do.
  4. Open a new chat inside the Project and test it with a real task.
  5. Update the documents whenever your offers or voice change.

Try this:

Prompt
Based on the documents in this project, write a short reply to this customer enquiry: [paste enquiry]. Use my voice. If you're missing information, ask me instead of guessing.
You're done when

A chat inside your Project writes in your voice and gets your offers right without you re-explaining anything.

Watch out

Keep passwords, API keys and private client details out of the Project.

Step 4 of 10

Explore connectors, APIs, MCPs, skills and plugins

Connect Claude to the tools you already use, like your email, calendar and docs. Add skills and plugins so it can do more, and see how APIs and MCPs open up even more.

How to do it

  1. Start with connectors. Connect one tool you use every day, such as your email, calendar or documents.
  2. Run a small, read-only task first, such as "summarize my meetings tomorrow" or "find the last email from [client]".
  3. Add a skill. A skill is a saved set of instructions Claude follows the same way every time. Start with one workflow you repeat weekly.
  4. Try a plugin if one fits a job you do, so Claude can do more in a focused area.
  5. Learn what APIs and MCPs are. They are the ways software talks to Claude and the way Claude is connected to other tools. You do not need to master them yet: just know they exist and what they unlock.

Try this:

Prompt
Look at my calendar for tomorrow and my last 24 hours of email. Give me a short list: the meetings, the emails that need a reply from me, and anything that looks urgent. Don't send or change anything.
TermIn plain words
ConnectorLets Claude look inside a tool you use, such as email, calendar or documents
SkillA saved set of instructions Claude follows the same way every time
PluginAn add-on that gives Claude extra abilities for a particular kind of work
APIA way for one piece of software to talk to another, including to Claude
MCPA standard way to connect Claude to other tools and data

You can see these in action in the SOP for building ten business agents inside Claude.

You're done when

Claude reads from at least one of your real tools and gives you a useful answer, and you have one skill saved.

Watch out

Start with read-only tasks. Turn on actions like sending or deleting only when you trust the result, and keep approvals switched on.

Step 5 of 10

Automate one repetitive job

Pick one job you do over and over, such as handling enquiries or preparing follow-ups. Let Claude do the draft and keep a human check at the end.

How to do it

  1. List the jobs you repeat each week. Pick one.
  2. Write down the steps you take, and what a good result looks like.
  3. Turn that into a prompt or a skill, and run it by hand several times on real examples.
  4. Make it produce drafts only: a drafted reply, a drafted follow-up. You review and send.
  5. Once it is reliably good, schedule it to run on its own.

Try this:

Prompt
Here is how I handle new enquiries: [your steps]. For this enquiry, [paste], draft a reply that follows those steps in my voice. Flag anything you're unsure about, and don't invent prices or promises.
You're done when

One job runs on a routine, drafts land ready for review, and you only have to check and send.

Watch out

Never let it send to customers without your approval until you have reviewed its output for a few weeks.

Step 6 of 10

Build your first tiny tool

Think of something simple, like a calculator, a client tracker or a landing page. Get a basic version working before you add more.

How to do it

  1. Pick one tool with one clear job.
  2. Describe what it should do, who uses it and what it should show. Keep the first version small.
  3. Ask Claude to build it, then open it and test it with real examples.
  4. Fix what is wrong, one change at a time.
  5. Only add features once the basic version works.

Try this:

Prompt
Build a simple [quote calculator / client tracker / landing page] as a single web page. It should [what it does]. Keep it clean and simple. Tell me how to open it and test it.
You're done when

A basic version works and you have used it on something real.

Watch out

Test with examples where you already know the right answer.

Step 7 of 10

Meet Claude Code

You don't need to be a developer. Describe what you want in plain English and let Claude Code build it with you, step by step.

How to do it

  1. Use a paid Claude plan and the Claude desktop app.
  2. Open the Code tab and give it a new, empty folder.
  3. Ask it to interview you and save a CLAUDE.md file, so it knows who you are.
  4. Give it a small project, such as the tiny tool from Step 6.
  5. Read each request before you approve it.

Try this:

Prompt
Interview me about who I am, what I'm working on and how I like things done. Then save the answers as a CLAUDE.md file in this folder.

The full walkthrough, including eight projects to try, is in the Claude Code starter guide.

You're done when

Claude Code has built something in your folder and you understand the changes you approved.

Step 8 of 10

Build your first AI agent

Give it one specific job, a few tools and clear boundaries. Test how it behaves when things go wrong, then improve it.

How to do it

  1. Choose one job, such as sorting incoming enquiries or preparing a weekly summary.
  2. Give it a few tools only, the ones that job needs.
  3. Write clear boundaries: what it may do, what needs your approval and what it must never do.
  4. Test the bad cases on purpose: missing information, an angry customer, a tool that fails. Check that it says so instead of guessing.
  5. Fix what breaks, then test again.

Try this:

Prompt
You are an assistant with one job: [the job]. You can use: [the few tools]. You may: [allowed actions]. You must ask me before: [actions that need approval]. You must never: [hard limits]. If information is missing or a tool fails, say so. Don't guess.

A tested set of ten agents, with their rules and test checklists, is in the business agents SOP.

You're done when

Your agent does its one job well, and handles failures safely.

Step 9 of 10

Study a product people love

Understand why customers use it. Rebuild one small feature for practice, then put your own spin on it.

How to do it

  1. Pick a product people love, ideally one close to your market.
  2. Find out why customers use it. Read reviews and comments, and note the job it does for them.
  3. Choose one small feature that you think matters most.
  4. Rebuild just that feature, with Claude or Claude Code.
  5. Then add your own spin: what would make it better for your customers?

Try this:

Prompt
I'm studying [product]. Based on these customer reviews: [paste]. What job are people hiring it to do, what do they love, and what do they complain about? Suggest one small feature I could rebuild for practice, and one way to make it better for [my customers].
You're done when

You have rebuilt one small feature and written down your own improvement.

Step 10 of 10

Build 3 to 5 demos

Make each demo solve a clear problem. Let someone try it and use their feedback to improve it.

How to do it

  1. Pick three to five problems that real people in your market have.
  2. Build one demo for each, keeping every demo small.
  3. Let someone try each demo while you watch. Don't explain it.
  4. Note where they get stuck or confused.
  5. Use that feedback to improve the demo, then show the next person.

Try this:

Prompt
I'm testing a demo of [what it does] with [type of person]. Write five short questions I can ask them after they try it, to find out what was confusing, what they liked and whether they would use it.
You're done when

You have three to five demos, each tried by someone and improved from their feedback.
