How a large language model is built, step by step

Large language models (LLMs) are the chatbots behind ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama. This SOP walks through how one is made, in the order it happens, from raw internet text to a working assistant.

It is based on a Stanford lecture on building LLMs. It is an overview: the goal is to understand every stage and what matters most, not to write code.

The five components

ComponentThe question it answersCovered here?
ArchitectureWhat shape of neural network do we use?No. All LLMs use transformers or a version of them. Plenty of material exists online
Training loss and algorithmHow do we actually train it?Yes (Steps 1, 2, 9, 10)
DataWhat do we train it on?Yes (Step 4)
EvaluationHow do we know we are making progress?Yes (Steps 5, 11, 12)
SystemsHow do we run it on real hardware?Yes (Step 13)
The big idea

Most academic work focuses on architecture and losses. In practice, what matters most is data, evaluation and systems, which is where industry puts its effort.

The two phases

PhaseWhat it doesEra
Pre-trainingTrains the model to model all of the internet: predict the next wordThe GPT-2 and GPT-3 era
Post-trainingTurns that model into an AI assistant that follows instructionsThe ChatGPT era

The pipeline at a glance

  1. Pre-training (Steps 1 to 7): define the task and loss, build the tokenizer, collect and clean data, evaluate, use scaling laws to plan, and work out the cost.
  2. Post-training (Steps 8 to 12): supervised fine-tuning, then learning from human preferences, then evaluating an assistant.
  3. Systems (Step 13): make all of it run efficiently on GPUs.

Part 1

Pre-training

Teach the model to predict the next piece of text across a huge slice of the internet.

Step 1

Define the task: language modeling

A language model is a model of the probability of a sequence of words (tokens). Given a sentence, it says how likely that sentence is to be written by a human.

SentenceWhat the model should know
The mouse ate the cheeseLikely. This is the baseline
The mouse ate cheeseLess likely. It has a grammar mistake, so the model needs some syntax knowledge
The cheese ate the mouseLess likely. Cheese does not eat mice, so the model needs some meaning knowledge

Autoregressive models

Current LLMs are autoregressive. They split the probability of a sentence into the probability of the first word, times the probability of the second word given the first, and so on. This is just the chain rule of probability, so nothing is approximated. In short: predict the next word given everything before it.

Because the model is a probability distribution, you can sample from it. That is why LLMs are called generative.

Downside

Generating text is a loop: produce a word, feed it back in, produce the next. Longer outputs take longer. This is one downside of the current approach, but it is what we have.

How one word is generated

  1. Tokenize the text into pieces and give each piece an ID.
  2. Pass the IDs through the model (the transformer).
  3. Get a probability distribution over the next token.
  4. Sample one token from that distribution.
  5. Detokenize the new ID back into text.

Only steps 4 and 5 are needed at inference time. During training you simply compare the model's prediction with the real next token.

Good to know

The model's output is one probability per token in the vocabulary, so vocabulary size equals output size. This is why you basically cannot add new tokens after training, and why the tokenizer matters so much (Step 3).

Step 2

Set the training loss

Training is a simple classification task: predict the next token.

  1. Embed each token into a vector.
  2. Pass the vectors through the transformer to get a representation of the whole context.
  3. Pass that through a linear layer so there is one output per token in the vocabulary.
  4. Apply a softmax to get probabilities.
  5. Compare to the real next word (a "one-hot" target: 100% on the real token) using cross-entropy loss.
  6. Update the weights to raise the probability of the real token and lower all the others.
Why this works

Minimizing this loss is the same as maximizing the likelihood of the text the model sees. Same goal, two ways to say it.

Step 3

Build the tokenizer

Tokenizers are rarely discussed but extremely important. They turn text into the pieces the model reads.

Why not words, and why not characters?

OptionProblem
One token per wordA typo has no token. Languages like Thai have no spaces between words. Tokens need to be more general than words
One token per characterIt would work, but sequences become very long, and cost grows quadratically with sequence length

Tokenizers sit in between: common sequences of characters get one token. On average, a token is about three to four letters.

Byte pair encoding (BPE): one of the two most common methods

  1. Start with a large text corpus (this is only for the tokenizer, not yet the LLM).
  2. Give every character its own token.
  3. Find the most common pair of neighbouring tokens and merge it into a new token.
  4. Repeat. For example "t" + "o" becomes "to", then "to" + "k" and so on until you get "token".
  5. In real life, do this on a much larger corpus. Example: "tokenizer" splits into "token" and "izer".

Rules to remember

  • Keep the small tokens after merging, so typos and rare words can still be built from characters.
  • Always apply the largest matching token. If "token" exists, use it rather than "t", "o", "k"...
  • A pre-tokenizer first splits on spaces and punctuation. This is just a speed optimization, since training a tokenizer considers every pair of tokens.
  • Each token has one unique ID. The word "bank" (money or river) gets the same token. The transformer, not the tokenizer, works out the meaning from context.
Where tokenizers hurt

Numbers: a number like 327 may be a single token, so the model does not see digits the way we do, which makes maths harder. Code: Python's four-space indents used to be handled badly, so the model struggled with code. Changing how code is tokenized was one of the big changes in GPT-4. Some researchers want to move to characters or bytes once new architectures can handle long sequences.

Step 4

Collect and clean the data

"Train on all of the internet" hides an enormous amount of work. The raw internet is dirty and unrepresentative. A random web page is mostly boilerplate and half-finished sentences.

The data pipeline

  1. Download the internet. Use a web crawler. Most teams use Common Crawl, which adds new pages every month. It holds roughly 250 billion pages, about 1 petabyte of data.
  2. Extract the text from the HTML. Hard parts: extracting maths, and removing repeated headers and footers.
  3. Filter undesirable content. Remove unsafe, harmful and personal information (PII). Companies keep long website blacklists, and train small classifiers to spot PII.
  4. Deduplicate. Remove repeated headers and footers, different URLs showing the same page, and paragraphs from common books copied thousands of times. Hard to do at scale.
  5. Heuristic filtering. Remove low-quality documents with rules: unusual token distributions, extremely long words, pages with three words, or pages with ten million.
  6. Model-based filtering. Take the pages linked from Wikipedia as a sign of quality. Train a simple classifier to tell "referenced by Wikipedia" from "random web", and keep more of the former.
  7. Classify by domain and re-weight. Sort data into entertainment, books, code and so on. Up-weight some (code and books, since more code seems to help reasoning) and down-weight others (entertainment).
  8. Finish on high-quality data. At the end of training, lower the learning rate and train on very high-quality data such as Wikipedia and human-written data. This effectively overfits on the good stuff.
  9. Optional: continue pre-training to support longer context.
Why harmful content is removed here, not penalized

Pre-training just aims to model how humans write. Teaching the model what not to say comes later, in post-training (Part 2).

Team and compute

Data work needs many people and lots of CPUs. For a team like Llama's (around 70 people), the lecturer guessed about 15 work on data. The data team is probably bigger than the team tuning the model itself.

How much data

Model or datasetTraining data size
Early academic benchmarksAbout 150 billion tokens (about 800 GB)
Llama 22 trillion tokens
Llama 315 trillion tokens
GPT-4Not public. Probably the same order of magnitude, around 13 trillion tokens if leaks are true

15 trillion tokens is roughly 100 to 1,000 times less than Common Crawl, which shows how much gets filtered out.

What is still unsolved

  • Processing data efficiently at scale.
  • Balancing the different domains.
  • Synthetic (AI-generated) data. There is not enough text on the internet.
  • Using multimodal data (images, audio) and whether it improves text performance.
  • Secrecy and copyright. Companies rarely say how they collect data, partly for competitive reasons and partly because of copyright liability.

Step 5

Evaluate the pre-trained model

Method 1: perplexity

Perplexity is the validation loss made easier to read. Take the average loss per token, then exponentiate it. The average makes it independent of sequence length, and exponentiating puts it in "vocabulary" units. In effect: perplexity = 2 to the power of the average loss.

  • The best possible score is 1 (perfect prediction). The worst is the vocabulary size (no idea at all).
  • Intuition: perplexity is roughly the number of tokens the model is hesitating between.
  • On a standard dataset it fell from about 70 tokens in 2017 to under 10 in 2023.
Limits

Perplexity depends on the tokenizer (a bigger vocabulary has a worse upper bound) and on the data used, so academics no longer use it for benchmarking. It is still important when developing your own LLM.

Method 2: aggregate many benchmarks

Collect as many automatically scoreable tasks as possible, mostly question answering, and combine the results. Two common collections are HELM (Stanford) and the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard.

Example: MMLU, a large set of multiple-choice questions across subjects such as college medicine, physics and astronomy. There are two ways to score it:

  1. Compare how likely the model is to generate each of the four answers, and check whether the real answer is the most likely. No text is generated.
  2. Ask the model to pick A, B, C or D, and check only those four tokens.

Two evaluation challenges

ChallengeWhat happens
Inconsistent methodsDifferent organizations scored MMLU differently. Llama 65B got 63.7% on HELM but 48.8% on another setup. Prompting adds more variation
Train-test contaminationThe test questions may have been in the training data. Academics cannot see the training data, so this is a real problem for them; companies know what they trained on. One trick: if the model finds the test set more likely in its original order than a shuffled order, it probably saw it in training

Step 6

Plan with scaling laws

Scaling laws are among the most useful tools in LLM development.

The core finding

More data and bigger models give better performance, and overfitting does not show up the way it does in a normal machine-learning class. Plotted on log-log axes, test loss falls along a straight line as compute, data or model size increase (shown in OpenAI's scaling laws paper). That makes progress predictable. There is no sign yet of this plateauing, though most people expect it eventually.

Use 1: choose a plan for your compute budget

Imagine you have 10,000 GPUs for 30 days. Which model do you train?

PipelineHow it worksProblem
OldTrain 30 models for one day each, pick the bestThe final model only ever trained for one day
NewFind a scaling recipe, tune on small models of different sizes (about 3 days), fit a scaling law, extrapolate, then train the final big model for about 27 daysNone of note. The big run is the real run

Example: transformer or LSTM? Train both at several small sizes, fit a line to each and extrapolate. Transformers came out better. When you compare lines, look at both the slope (scaling rate) and the intercept: a model can start worse and win at larger scale.

What this says about priorities

Small architecture tweaks only shift the intercept a little, so they are secondary. Data quality changes the scaling curve much more.

Use 2: train a bigger model or use more data?

The Chinchilla paper answered this by training models at fixed compute (called isoFLOPs) with different mixes of model size and data, picking the best of each, and fitting a line through the winners.

  • Compute-optimal rule: about 20 tokens per parameter. Add a parameter, add 20 tokens of training data.
  • That rule only minimizes training cost. Real companies also pay for inference (answering users), so smaller models trained longer are cheaper to run. Including inference costs, the ratio is closer to 150 tokens per parameter, which is about where today's production models are.
  • Inference is very expensive at scale, but is a separate topic.

Other questions scaling laws can answer

  • Which data and which data mixture to use.
  • Which architecture, and whether wider or deeper.
  • Whether to pay for more GPUs or collect more data.
The bitter lesson

Compute keeps getting cheaper and more models improve with scale. So the winning approach is architectures and systems that use compute well, plus good data. Do the simple things well and scale them, rather than over-complicating the design.

Part 2

Post-training

Turn a model that predicts internet text into an assistant that follows instructions.

Step 8

Understand why post-training is needed

A pure language model completes text the way the internet would. Ask GPT-3 to "explain the moon landing to a six-year-old" and you might get "explain the theory of gravity to a six-year-old", because on the internet one question is often followed by more questions, not an answer.

The goal of post-training (also called alignment) is to make the model:

  • Follow instructions given by users.
  • Follow the designers' wishes, such as moderation: refusing to write something toxic.

What you want is human-written questions with ideal answers. That data is expensive and hard to find, while pre-training data is plentiful but not what you want. So the plan is: pre-train on everything, then fine-tune a little on the data you really want. Because the model already knows language, very little fine-tuning data is needed.

Step 9

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

SFT means continuing to train the model, with the same next-word loss, on answers that humans wrote for real questions. This is the step that moved GPT-3 (known to researchers) to ChatGPT (known to everyone).

How to do it

  1. Collect question-and-answer pairs written by humans.
  2. Fine-tune the pre-trained model on them with the same language-modeling loss.
  3. Use a higher learning rate than at the end of pre-training. Different hyperparameters are one reason this is called "post"-training.
  4. Update all the weights in industry. In open source, LoRA is common: it only adds small changes to each layer's output.

Pre-training is best viewed as the initialization of the weights. After that, SFT is just training again.

Cutting the cost of human data

The Alpaca project used 175 human-written question-answer pairs, asked the best model at the time to generate 52,000 similar ones, and fine-tuned Llama 7B on them. The results were pretty good. Synthetic data is now a big field.

You don't need much data

The LIMA paper found that going from 2,000 to 32,000 SFT examples did not help much. The reason: SFT does not teach new knowledge. A pre-trained model has seen every kind of writer on the internet. SFT just tells it to copy one type of writer, mainly the format of a good answer.

Open question

Training only on AI-generated data for several generations probably stops improving. The lecturer expects the best approach to combine humans and models: the model drafts, humans make quick edits, which is much faster than writing from scratch.

Step 10

Learn from human preferences (RLHF)

Why SFT alone is not enough

  • Limited by human ability. People can usually tell which of two answers is better, even if they could not write the best one themselves.
  • It may cause hallucination. If a human writes a correct fact the model never learned, the model learns to produce plausible-sounding text instead of only what it knows. This is a hypothesis.
  • It is expensive to have humans write full ideal answers.

So instead of copying humans, maximize human preference.

The preference data

  1. For each instruction, have an already fine-tuned model produce two answers.
  2. Ask a labeler which one is better.
  3. Train the model to produce more of the preferred kind and less of the other.

Route A: reinforcement learning with PPO

This is what the original ChatGPT used. It needs a reward to optimize.

  • Binary reward: +1 if better than a baseline, -1 if not. Too sparse: it does not say by how much.
  • Reward model: train a classifier on the human preferences (the Bradley-Terry model). Its continuous score says roughly how much better one answer is.
  1. Do SFT.
  2. Train a reward model on human preferences.
  3. Optimize the LLM with PPO against that reward, with a regularization term so it does not over-optimize a reward model that is not a perfect copy of human taste.
  4. Repeat: collect new data and run again.
Costs of PPO

It is messy in practice: rollouts, outer loops, clipping, and poorly documented details. Also, a PPO-trained model is a policy, not a model of text probabilities any more, so it does not give meaningful likelihoods (see Step 12).

Route B: DPO, the simpler option

Direct Preference Optimization skips reinforcement learning. It simply raises the probability of the answer people liked and lowers the probability of the one they did not. Under some assumptions, it has the same best solution as PPO, but is just maximum likelihood, so it is much simpler. It performs about as well as PPO in the results shown, is the standard in the open-source community and, the lecturer believes, in industry too.

One possible advantage of the reward-model route: it can use unlabelled data, because the reward model labels it. In practice this has given no gains.

StageWhat improves
Pre-trained onlyOkay, and improves with scale
After SFTA little better
After RLHF (PPO or DPO)Better again. Often beats human reference answers on tasks like summarization. PPO and DPO score the same

Typical data amounts: SFT uses roughly 5,000 to 50,000 examples, RLHF around 1 million, still far less than pre-training.

Step 11

Collect preference data (humans and LLMs)

Problems with human labelers

  • Slow and expensive.
  • Hard to judge what matters. People favour surface features like length over correctness. Humans nearly always pick the longer answer. This is why RLHF makes chatbots longer-winded.
  • Low agreement. Humans agreed with the majority only about 66% of the time on a two-way choice. The authors labelled data themselves, after discussing the rules for three hours, and got only 67 to 68%.
  • Annotator distribution. Who the labelers are shapes whose preferences the model reflects.
  • Ethics. Crowd workers are often paid poorly and see a lot of toxic content.

The fix: use an LLM as the labeler

Replace human preferences with an LLM's preferences. In the lecturer's experiment, human labels cost about $300 per 10,000 examples (on Mechanical Turk). LLM labels were about 50 times cheaper and agreed with the majority of humans more than individual humans did, because models have no variance, only some bias. Many teams now combine both.

Step 12

Evaluate an assistant

Evaluating something like ChatGPT is hard. There is no single right answer, and there are many question types: open questions, writing, summarizing and more.

Why the old methods failDetail
Validation lossNot comparable between methods, for example PPO and DPO
PerplexityAligned models are not calibrated. A PPO model drifts toward a single best answer, so a different but equally good answer can get close to zero likelihood
Fixed benchmarksQuestions are open-ended, so it is hard to automate

Option 1: Chatbot Arena

Random internet users chat with two anonymous bots, then say which answer is better. Hundreds of thousands of votes produce a ranking. It is the most trusted benchmark. Limits: users skew technical, so questions lean toward software and AI tools, and it is too slow and costly to use during development.

Option 2: LLM as the judge (AlpacaEval)

  1. For each instruction, generate an answer from a baseline model and one from the model you are testing.
  2. Ask a strong model (such as GPT-4) which is better.
  3. Average over the whole set to get a win rate, then rank models.

Results: 98% correlation with Chatbot Arena, under 3 minutes, under $10 to run.

Watch out for length bias

LLM judges prefer longer outputs, and models trained against them keep drifting longer. GPT-4 won 50% against itself by default, but 64.4% when told to be verbose and only about 20% when told to be concise. The fix used: a regression (causal inference) that controls for length, which cut the effect a lot.

Part 3

Systems

Compute is the biggest bottleneck, so efficient use of hardware matters.

Step 13

Make it run efficiently on GPUs

Why not just buy more GPUs? They are expensive and scarce (even with $10 million you cannot buy the best), and with many GPUs, the time spent communicating between them adds up. Efficient use of what you have is essential.

GPU basics

  • GPUs are built for throughput (one command across many cores on different data). CPUs are built for latency.
  • GPUs are optimized for matrix multiplication. If you can express work that way, it is about 10 times faster.
  • Compute is improving faster than memory and communication. Data struggles to reach the processors, so GPUs often sit idle unless code is optimized.
  • There is a memory hierarchy: close to the cores is fast but small; far away is large but slow.

The key metric: model FLOP utilization (MFU)

Observed throughput divided by the GPU's theoretical maximum. Reaching 50% is considered very good. Llama's training was around 45%.

Trick 1: low precision

Fewer bits per number means less to move, faster communication and less memory. For deep learning, tiny decimal differences do not matter because training is already noisy.

  1. Store the weights in 32 bits.
  2. Just before the computation, convert to 16 bits.
  3. Do the fast computation in 16 bits.
  4. Update the weights in 32 bits, so very small learning-rate updates still register.

This is called automatic mixed precision.

Trick 2: operator fusion

By default, every line of PyTorch sends data from the GPU's main memory to the processor and back. Two operations in a row means two round trips. Fusing them sends the data once, runs all operations, and sends it back once. In PyTorch, torch.compile does this by rewriting your code in C++ and CUDA, and can make a model around 2 times faster.

Also important but not covered: tiling, parallelization and mixture of experts.

Wrap-up

Key takeaways

  1. LLMs predict the next token. Everything else is built on that.
  2. Data, evaluation and systems matter more in practice than architecture tweaks.
  3. Tokenization quietly causes problems with numbers and code.
  4. Data cleaning is a huge, mostly unsolved amount of work, and the key for many companies.
  5. Scaling laws let you predict results from small experiments and plan big runs.
  6. Pre-training gives knowledge. Post-training mostly gives format and behavior.
  7. Human preferences can be expensive and biased, so teams now use LLMs as judges and labelers, while watching for length bias.
  8. Compute is the bottleneck, so GPU efficiency matters.

Where to learn more

The lecture points to three Stanford courses: CS224N (background and history), CS324 (large language models, in-depth readings) and CS336 (build your own LLM from scratch, with a very heavy workload). Remember

Pre-train for knowledge. Post-train for behavior. Evaluate honestly. Spend your effort on data, evaluation and systems.
